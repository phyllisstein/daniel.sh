A gentle note on what you're looking at

OKLCh describes a colour the way you'd describe it out loud. How light is it (L), how colourful (C), and which hue (H). Its virtue is that equal numeric steps look like equal perceptual steps — which is exactly what you want from a palette ramp, and exactly what HSL fails to give you.

Chroma has a ceiling, and the ceiling moves

Here is the part that trips everyone up. L and H can be set freely, but C cannot: the set of colours a screen can actually show is a lumpy, asymmetric solid, not a box. So the maximum chroma available depends on the lightness and the hue you already chose. Yellow reaches its most vivid when it's light; blue when it's dark. Ask for a vivid pale yellow and you're fine; ask for an equally vivid pale blue and you are asking for a colour that does not exist.

Near white and near black the ceiling collapses toward zero — a colour one shade off white cannot be saturated, because there's almost no room between it and the white point. That's why maximising chroma is safe at the ends of a ramp: it barely moves the tints, and does its real work in the middle.

What the browser does when you overshoot

An oklch() outside the display's gamut isn't an error. Per CSS Color 4 the browser gamut-maps it, walking the chroma down until the colour becomes displayable. That sounds benign, and for one colour it is. The trouble is that it happens per colour, independently: several neighbouring steps that all overshot get walked down onto nearly the same reachable colour, and a ramp that was carefully spaced quietly collapses into flat bands. Look at the middle lane of the blues, or the reds, with the surround set to paper — the steps stop stepping.

Why the corrected ramps look calmer

The bottom lane keeps each colour's lightness and hue exactly as the sRGB source had them, and pushes chroma to the boundary and no further. Nothing needs mapping, so nothing collapses. On a P3 display you get real extra vividness through the midtones; on an sRGB display the mapping has a very short distance to travel and lands close to the original — which is why the top and bottom lanes can look so similar here and still differ on a wide-gamut screen.

And a caveat about your own eyes

Colour appearance is relative. The same swatch shifts noticeably against black, mid-grey and white — that's simultaneous contrast, and it's why the surround toggle is at the top of this page rather than buried. Colourists judge against neutral mid-grey for exactly this reason. If two lanes look identical on the dark setting, try paper before concluding they match.
